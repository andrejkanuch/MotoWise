If you want to know how to start motorcycle maintenance , the honest answer is simpler than most first year riders expect: learn a two minute pre ride check, keep your oil and chain on schedule, and treat your owner's manual as the final word on every number. Do those three things and you'll catch nearly every problem before it strands you — or worse. This guide walks a brand new rider through the exact routine, the five jobs worth learning first, and a first year schedule you can actually follow. You don't need a lift, a torque wrench collection, or a mechanic's background. You need a habit. MotoVault can track this whole schedule for you and remind you before each service is due — but the routine below works with nothing more than your manual and ten minutes. Start with your owner's manual — it outranks every blog Every make, model, and year is slightly different. Oil capacity, chain slack, tire pressure, and service intervals are set by the people who built your bike, and those figures live in your owner's manual (and often on a sticker on the swingarm, chain guard, or under the seat). Any range you read online — including this article — is a starting point for understanding, not a substitute for your specific manual. If you bought used and the manual is missing, most manufacturers publish free PDF versions on their websites. Download it before you touch anything. The 10 minute pre ride check (T CLOCS) The single most valuable habit in motorcycling is a pre ride inspection. The Motorcycle Safety Foundation (MSF) teaches a checklist called T CLOCS , and it's the framework taught in the Basic RiderCourse. The acronym covers six systems: T — Tires & Wheels: tread, pressure (checked cold), wheels, and brakes. C — Controls: levers, cables, hoses, and throttle (it should snap closed on its own). L — Lights & Electrics: headlight, brake light, turn signals, battery, and switches. O — Oil & Other Fluids: engine oil, coolant, brake and clutch fluid, fuel — levels and leaks. C — Chassis: frame, suspension, chain or belt, and fasteners. S — Stands: side and center stand condition and springs. You won't inspect all of it every single ride, but a quick scan of tires, lights, oil level, and chain before you roll takes about two minutes and prevents the most common breakdowns. As MSF puts it, doing this regularly "helps you discover small issues before they become big ones." The five jobs every new rider should learn You can pay a shop for everything, but these five are cheap, teach you your machine, and are hard to get wrong once you've done them once. 1. Oil and filter changes Oil is the cheapest insurance your engine will ever get. Two intervals matter: The first (break in) change comes early — many manufacturers call for it around 1,000 km / ~600 miles on a new bike to flush out fine metal from break in. If you just bought new, don't skip this one. (More on this in our motorcycle break in period guide.) Routine changes for most street bikes fall in the ~5,000 km / ~3,000 mile to ~8,000 km / ~5,000 mile range, or at least once a year , whichever comes first. Mineral oils sit at the shorter end; full synthetics can go longer. Your manual sets your number. Change the oil warm (it drains cleaner), on level ground, and replace the filter at the same time. Time riding matters as much as distance — oil degrades with heat cycles and moisture even if you barely ride, which is why the annual change matters for low mileage bikes. 2. Chain: clean, lube, and adjust If your bike is chain driven (most are), this is your most frequent job. A dry or over tight chain wears sprockets fast and, at the extreme, can lock a rear wheel. Clean and lube every ~500–1,000 km / ~300–600 miles , and always after riding in rain or dust. Harley Davidson and Haynes both put routine chain care in this range for street riders. Lube the inside of the chain while slowly rotating the rear wheel, so centrifugal force carries it into the rollers. Wipe off the excess. Check slack at the tightest point. Manuals typically specify somewhere in the 20–35 mm / ~0.8–1.4 in window, but this varies by bike — use your manual's figure, not a generic one. Too tight is worse than slightly loose. Note: if your bike has a belt drive, clean it but never lubricate a belt . 3. Tire pressure and condition Under inflation is the most common — and most dangerous — neglected item on a beginner's bike. It ruins handling, causes uneven wear, and raises blowout risk. Check pressure cold , meaning the bike has sat for about three hours or hasn't been ridden more than a mile or two. Riding heats the air and gives a falsely high reading. Use the pressure in your owner's manual or the bike's placard (swingarm, chain guard, or under the seat) — not the "Max PSI" stamped on the tire sidewall. As Michelin and Bridgestone both stress, the sidewall number is the tire's ceiling, not your target. Inspect the tread and sidewalls for wear bars, cracks, embedded objects, and bulges every time you check pressure. 4. Brakes You don't need to rebuild calipers, but you should know two things: your pads and your fluid . Look at pad thickness through the caliper; if the friction material is getting thin, book a change. Check the brake fluid level in the reservoir sight glass and note that fluid should be replaced periodically (many manuals say every two years) because it absorbs moisture over time. Each brake, used alone, should firmly hold the bike from rolling. 5. Controls, cables, and lights Squeeze every lever and roll the throttle: motion should be smooth and the throttle should snap shut on its own . Lubricate cable ends and pivots if they feel sticky. Then walk around and confirm the headlight (high and low), brake light (on both front and rear brake application), and all four turn signals actually work. These take 60 seconds and are the difference between being seen and being invisible. A simple first year maintenance schedule Use this as a mental model, then override every figure with your manual. The starter toolkit You can begin with very little: A quality tire pressure gauge (the built in gas station ones are often inaccurate). Chain cleaner, a soft brush, and chain lube suited to your chain type (O/X ring vs. standard). Basic metric sockets and hex/Torx keys to fit your bike. An oil drain pan, funnel, and the right oil filter for oil changes. A rear/paddock stand so the wheel spins freely for chain work. Your owner's manual — and, if you want step by step depth, a model specific service manual (see our best motorcycle maintenance books for 2026). Add a torque wrench when you're ready to do fastener work — torque specs matter, and guessing on brake or axle bolts is exactly where beginners get into trouble. Common beginner mistakes to avoid Riding on the sidewall max pressure. It's a ceiling, not a target. Over tightening the chain. A too tight chain stresses the countershaft bearing and can bind the suspension. Skipping the first oil change on a new bike to save money — it's the most important one. Only counting miles. Oil and brake fluid age with time and heat cycles, so the annual/biennial clock matters even for low mileage bikes. Guessing torque values instead of looking them up. When a number is safety critical, verify it against your manual.
