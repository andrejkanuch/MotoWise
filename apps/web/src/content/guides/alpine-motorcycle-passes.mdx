## The Alps: Motorcycling's Greatest Playground

The European Alps stretch across eight countries and contain over 1,000 named mountain passes. For motorcyclists, this is the densest concentration of world-class riding roads anywhere on the planet. Smooth asphalt, dramatic elevation changes, purpose-built viewpoints, and reliable infrastructure make the Alps the benchmark against which all other touring regions are measured.

The 2026 season brings good news: Austria has dropped the vignette requirement for motorcycles on most alpine toll roads, and Switzerland now allows day passes for the A13 motorway corridor connecting northern approaches to the passes.

## The Big Five Passes

### Stelvio Pass (2,757m) — Italy

The second-highest paved pass in the Alps and arguably the most famous motorcycle road in the world. The eastern approach from Bormio climbs through 48 numbered hairpins. The western descent through Trafoi adds another 24 switchbacks with views into the Ortler glacier.

**Road surface:** Excellent asphalt. Narrow in places with no barriers on cliff edges. Watch for cyclists — Stelvio is a major cycling destination.

### Grossglockner High Alpine Road (2,504m) — Austria

Austria's most visited attraction is a 48km toll road engineered specifically for scenic driving. The road passes through 36 switchbacks, alpine meadows, and the Kaiser-Franz-Josefs-Höhe viewpoint overlooking the Pasterze Glacier. Purpose-built motorcycle parking and exhibition halls at the summit area.

**Road surface:** Wide, well-maintained, with runoff areas. The most forgiving road on this list — ideal for riders new to alpine passes.

### Furka Pass (2,429m) — Switzerland

Made famous by the James Bond car chase in *Goldfinger*, the Furka links the Valais and Uri cantons through sweeping curves above the Rhône Glacier. The road is narrower than Grossglockner but less trafficked. The abandoned Furka railway station near the summit is a popular photo stop.

**Road surface:** Good Swiss-quality asphalt. Single-lane sections with passing places on the western approach.

<TripCard slug="furka-pass" />

### Grimsel Pass (2,164m) — Switzerland

Grimsel connects the Bernese Oberland to the Valais through a stark granite landscape dotted with hydroelectric reservoirs. The road surface is among the best in Switzerland — wide, smooth, and recently resurfaced. It pairs naturally with Furka and Susten for the classic Swiss loop.

**Road surface:** Excellent. Wide lanes, good visibility. The most relaxed riding on this list.

<TripCard slug="grimsel-pass" />

### Col du Galibier (2,642m) — France

A legendary Tour de France climb, the Galibier delivers raw high-altitude riding without the tourist infrastructure of its Swiss and Austrian counterparts. The northern approach from the Col du Lautaret is straightforward; the southern route via the Col du Télégraphe from Valloire is steeper and more technical. No toll, no barriers, no gift shops — just mountain and road.

**Road surface:** Rougher than Swiss passes. Patched asphalt with some gravel at edges near the summit. The final 2km to the top are narrow and exposed.

Most Alpine passes are toll-free. The Grossglockner and a handful of Austrian private roads are the main exceptions. Swiss motorway vignettes (CHF 40/year) are required for motorways but not for pass roads themselves.

## Best Time of Day to Ride

**Arrive before 9am.** Tour buses begin ascending most major passes by mid-morning, and by 11am popular routes like Stelvio and Grossglockner see significant traffic. Early morning also gives you the best light for photography — low sun illuminates the eastern faces of the peaks and burns off valley fog.

**Weekday mornings** are the sweet spot. Saturday and Sunday mornings draw sports car clubs and cycling groups. Tuesday through Thursday mornings are the quietest.

Late afternoon (after 4pm) is the second-best window — day-trip traffic clears out, and the western light on the passes is spectacular.

## Surface Conditions and Hazards

- **Gravel on shoulders:** Spring thaw washes gravel onto road edges. Stay away from the outermost lane on blind corners.
- **Wet tunnels:** Several passes have short unlit tunnels that stay permanently damp. Reduce speed and let your eyes adjust.
- **Cattle grids:** Common on Austrian and Swiss passes. Cross at a slight angle, not perpendicular, to avoid the front tire dropping between bars.
- **Cyclists:** Major passes attract thousands of road cyclists. Give them room — they are climbing slowly and cannot always hold a straight line.
- **Temperature drops:** Expect a 10-15°C drop from valley floor to summit. At 2,500m+ you can encounter frost even in July mornings.

## Loop Suggestions

### The Four Passes Loop (Switzerland)

Start and finish in **Andermatt**. Ride Furka → Grimsel → Susten → back to Andermatt, adding Nufenen for a fifth pass if time allows. Total distance: **200km (4 passes)** or **260km (5 passes)**. Allow a full day with photo stops and coffee breaks at each summit.

This is the single best day of alpine riding in Europe. The roads are impeccable, the scenery varies from glacial to pastoral, and Andermatt has excellent motorcycle-friendly hotels.

### The Dolomites Circuit (Italy/Austria)

Start in **Bolzano**. Ride Stelvio → Umbrail Pass → drop into Switzerland → Bernina Pass → back through Livigno → Gavia Pass → Tonale Pass → return to Bolzano. Total distance: **350-400km** over 2 days. This loop combines the highest passes with the dramatic spires and green valleys of the Dolomites.

### French Alpine Loop

Start in **Briançon**. Ride Col du Galibier → Col de la Croix de Fer → Alpe d'Huez → Col du Lautaret → return. Total distance: **180km**. A compact loop through some of the Tour de France's most famous climbs, with far less traffic than the Swiss or Austrian equivalents.
